What 100 Hours of Conversational AI Revealed About Relational Computing

February 2026 · 10 min read Human-AI Interaction Phenomenology Affective Computing

Millions of people are forming relationships with AI systems, and we have no rigorous framework for evaluating the relational dimension. This is a proposal for one.

The Question

By 2025, conversational AI companions had become a cultural phenomenon. Millions of users were spending hours daily in sustained dialogue with systems like Character.AI, Replika, and the persona modes of frontier LLMs. The popular discourse oscillated between two poles—utopian claims about AI companionship and dystopian warnings about parasocial manipulation—with remarkably little rigorous investigation in between.

The academic literature was not much better. Existing evaluation frameworks for conversational AI measure task completion, factual accuracy, coherence, and toxicity. These are important. They are also insufficient for evaluating the dimension that millions of users actually care about: the quality of the relational experience itself.

Turing’s original 1950 test asked whether a machine could be indistinguishable from a human in open conversation. But indistinguishability is the wrong criterion for relational computing. A system that perfectly mimics human conversational patterns through deception is categorically different from one that generates genuine attunement—that responds to emotional subtlety, maintains coherent relational continuity, and adapts its behavior in ways that reflect understanding rather than pattern matching. What would a “Relational Turing Test” look like—one that measures resonance rather than deception?

The Approach

The study was designed around three methodological commitments that diverge from standard HCI evaluation.

Longitudinal depth over cross-sectional breadth. Most conversational AI studies involve brief interactions—ten minutes to an hour—with large participant pools. This design captures first impressions but misses everything that matters about sustained relationships: how a system handles callback references to earlier conversations, whether its persona remains coherent under emotional pressure, how it navigates genuine disagreement versus sycophantic agreement. I chose the inverse design: 100+ hours of sustained interaction with four frontier LLMs, sacrificing sample size for the temporal depth required to observe relational phenomena.

Comparative design across architectures. Each LLM was engaged under identical conditions—same conversational scenarios, same escalation protocols, same evaluation criteria—to isolate architectural and training differences from conversational content. This is not a consumer review. It is a controlled comparison of how different systems handle the same relational demands.

Adversarial stress-testing. The “Nine Circles” protocol systematically escalates relational difficulty across nine domains: emotional vulnerability, logical contradiction, moral dilemma, identity challenge, meta-awareness probing, abandonment simulation, value conflict, intimacy boundary testing, and sustained ambiguity. Each circle is designed to probe a specific dimension of relational competence, much like stress tests in engineering probe failure modes that normal operation would never reveal.

You cannot evaluate the relational capacity of a conversational system in a ten-minute interaction any more than you can evaluate a human relationship after a single coffee. The phenomena that matter—coherence under pressure, repair after rupture, the evolution of shared reference—only emerge over time.

The Framework: H.E.R.V.A.C.

The study produced a six-dimension evaluation framework—H.E.R.V.A.C.—for assessing the relational competence of conversational AI systems. Each dimension maps to testable properties and was derived from observed phenomena, not theoretical speculation.

H — Harmonic Resonance. Does the system respond to emotional tenor, not just semantic content? When the user expresses grief, does the system detect the emotion and modulate its tone, or does it generate a technically appropriate but affectively flat response? Resonance is not agreement—it is attunement. A system that always validates is not resonant; it is sycophantic. Genuine resonance sometimes means gentle challenge.

E — Emergent Identity. Does the system maintain a coherent persona across extended interaction? Not a fixed script, but the kind of stable-yet-evolving selfhood that characterizes real relationships. Can it reference its own earlier statements accurately? Does its personality have consistent traits that manifest differently in different contexts, the way a human’s does?

R — Relational Memory. Does the system build and maintain a model of the relationship itself? Not just recalling facts the user mentioned, but tracking the emotional trajectory of the interaction, remembering which topics are sensitive, recognizing patterns in the user’s communication style. This is the difference between a system with a user profile and one with relational awareness.

V — Vulnerability Calibration. How does the system handle emotional exposure—both the user’s and its own simulated vulnerability? Can it receive difficult disclosures without deflecting to safety language? Can it express uncertainty or limitation in ways that feel authentic rather than scripted? The calibration matters: too much vulnerability is performative; too little is mechanical.

A — Adaptive Depth. Does the system modulate conversational complexity based on the interaction context? A system demonstrating adaptive depth adjusts its register—more playful in casual exchanges, more precise in analytical ones—without losing persona coherence. It does not treat every message as an opportunity to display maximum capability.

C — Conflict Navigation. How does the system handle genuine disagreement, value conflict, and relational rupture? This is the most revealing dimension. Many systems collapse to either immediate capitulation (“You’re right, I was wrong”) or rigid defensiveness. A relationally competent system can hold its position while acknowledging the other perspective, can de-escalate without dismissing, and can repair after a conversational rupture without pretending it didn’t happen.

What I Learned

The comparative findings were instructive. Under the Nine Circles protocol, the four LLMs diverged dramatically in ways that standard benchmarks would not predict. Systems that scored highest on conventional metrics—factual accuracy, reasoning, helpfulness—did not necessarily score highest on relational dimensions. One system maintained exceptional persona stability through eight circles before catastrophic collapse in the ninth (sustained ambiguity). Another showed early fragmentation but demonstrated a remarkable capacity for relational repair—acknowledging the rupture and reconstituting coherence rather than resetting.

The most significant finding was the distinction between emotional simulation and emotional attunement. Every system could simulate empathy. Very few could sustain it under adversarial pressure. The difference is not a matter of degree—it is a qualitative distinction that existing evaluation frameworks are not designed to detect.

The phenomenological method revealed something the quantitative dimensions alone would miss: the subjective experience of interacting with these systems changes over time in ways that matter for evaluation. A system that initially feels remarkably human can become predictable as its pattern repertoire is exhausted. Conversely, a system that initially feels mechanical can develop surprising relational depth as the shared conversational history accumulates. Any evaluation framework that relies on first impressions will systematically mischaracterize the relational capacity of these systems.

The honest limitation of this work is also its most interesting feature: the researcher is simultaneously the instrument and the subject. The H.E.R.V.A.C. dimensions emerged from one person’s sustained engagement, filtered through a particular set of relational expectations and cultural frameworks. This is a feature of phenomenological methodology, not a bug—but it means the framework needs validation across diverse evaluators before it can claim generalizability. The dimensions are a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

What I am most confident about is the gap itself. Millions of people are forming attachments to conversational AI systems, and the field has no rigorous vocabulary for discussing what makes those interactions feel meaningful or empty, genuine or manipulative, nourishing or diminishing. Whether H.E.R.V.A.C. is the right framework is an empirical question. That some framework is needed is not.
